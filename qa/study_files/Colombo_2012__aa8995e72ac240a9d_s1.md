## Computational level
- **study_focus:** Norm learning; social reward effects on learning social norms of tipping
- **study_focus_short:** Norm learning; social reward effects on learning social norms of tipping
- **learning_mode_description:** - Learning mode: Learning from social/non-social feedback about a social norm of tipping   - Learning from:     - Source type (social): other (server/stranger via facial expression feedback) [Social condition] / Source type (non-social): world (tick/cross marks) [Non-social condition]     - Source content (social): outcome (approval/disapproval via happy/angry faces) [Social condition] / Source content (non-social): outcome (correct/incorrect feedback) [Non-social condition]   - Learning about:     - Target type (social): social structure (social norm of tipping)     - Target content (social): action/policy (appropriate tipping percentage)
- **task_description:** Participants played a "tipping game" in which they repeatedly dined at restaurants in a fictional foreign country and had to learn the local social norm of tipping (23% or 50% of the bill) across three blocks of 40 trials each, receiving either social feedback (happy/angry faces) or non-social feedback (tick/cross marks) after each tipping decision.
- **task_paradigm:** Norm learning
- **players:** Single agent (participant), single target (fictional server/social environment); between-subjects: social (N=20) vs. non-social (N=19) feedback condition
- **n_players:** network (5+)
- **partner_type:** human (recorded)
- **stimuli:** Restaurant service quality (good/bad), bill amounts, happy/angry facial expressions (JAFFE database) or tick/cross marks as feedback
- **method:** behavioural
- **method_full:** behavioural
- **main_result:** - Main Results:   - Participants in the social condition tipped significantly more than non-social condition (mean difference = 4.17%, p < 0.001 across blocks)   - Absolute difference between norm and amount tipped was significantly lower in social vs. non-social condition (mean difference = -4.17%, p < 0.001)   - Social condition participants made significantly faster decisions (mean difference = -749 ms, p < 0.001)   - More learners in social condition (12/20) vs. non-social condition (7/19)   - Social condition showed higher learning rate (alpha = 0.42 vs. 0.31) and lower inverse temperature (tau = 3.60 vs. 5.80)

## Quality
- **sample_size:** N=40 (20 social condition [7 females], 20 non-social condition [10 females]; 1 excluded from non-social for not understanding the task; final N=39); ages 19-37 (M=26.22, SD=4.31)
- **population_category:** healthy adults
- **population_age_range:** 19–37
- **ecological_validity:** Moderate — uses a tipping scenario with fictional currency that approximates a real social norm learning situation, but the interaction is with a computer and feedback is stochastic rather than from a real person. The "server" is not a real agent.
- **eligibility_flag:** Flag: thesis — only Chapter 7 (the tipping game experiment, joint work with Stankevicius and Series) qualifies as empirical study with computational modeling and human behavioral data. Chapters 1-6 are theoretical/philosophical. Also flag: the "social agent" providing feedback is an automated system (computer-generated faces), not a real social partner.
- **concerns:** - Only one model tested (no model comparison) - No simulation, parameter recovery, or model recovery reported - No formal effect sizes reported (only mean differences and p-values) - Social vs. emotional confound acknowledged by authors (facial expressions convey both social and emotional information) - Reward magnitudes not matched between conditions (happy face vs. tick mark may differ in intrinsic reward value) - High noise in feedback structure may explain generally poor learning performance - w_max value not specified - No formal goodness-of-fit statistics reported beyond mean difference between model and data
- **limitations_reported:** The stimuli used in the social condition did not help determine whether behavioural effects depended on social rather than only the emotional dimension of facial expressions; a third condition with emotional non-social reward outcomes would be needed; the reward structure made the learning task especially hard due to high noise in the mapping between state-action pairs and reward outcomes across blocks, and the reliability of feedback was independent from the distance between amount tipped and underlying social norm
- **limitations_categorized:** Social-emotional confound in stimuli; task difficulty/noise; limited ecological validity; no neural data
