## Computational level
- **study_focus:** Social reinforcement learning in social anxiety — how high vs. low socially anxious individuals learn differently from socially rewarding (happy faces) vs. punishing (angry faces) outcomes.
- **study_focus_short:** Social reinforcement learning in social anxiety
- **learning_mode_description:** - Learning mode: Learning from probabilistic social feedback (happy/angry facial expressions) about which social partners are rewarding vs. punishing   - Learning from:     - Source type (social): other (neutral face stimuli representing social partners)     - Source content (social): outcome (happy face = social reward; angry face = social punishment)   - Learning about:     - Target type (social): other (social partner's reward/punishment contingency)     - Target content (social): stimulus (reward probability of each face)
- **task_description:** Participants selected between pairs of neutral faces with varying probabilities (80/20, 70/30, 60/40) of changing to a happy (reward) or angry (punishment) face. In the training phase they learned through trial and error which face was more rewarding; in the testing phase faces were recombined into all possible pairs and participants chose the more rewarding face without feedback.
- **task_paradigm:** Probabilistic reward learning
- **players:** Single agent (participant), multi-target (6 face stimuli with varying reward contingencies)
- **n_players:** multi-target (3+)
- **partner_type:** human (recorded)
- **stimuli:** Static neutral face photographs (NimStim and FACES databases), happy and angry facial expression feedback; White faces, sex-matched to participant (male faces for female participants, female faces for male participants)
- **method:** behavioural
- **method_full:** behavioural
- **main_result:** - Main Results:   - No significant group differences in reward or punishment learning rates (SA Group: b = 0.00, CI [-0.02, 0.03], t = 0.45, p = .652; SA Group x PE Valence: b = 0.00, CI [-0.02, 0.02], t = 0.01, p = .994)   - Significant SA Group x Choice Type interaction on testing phase accuracy (OR = 1.17, CI [1.10, 1.25], t = 4.88, p < .001)   - High SA group: punishment learning accuracy (M = 0.78) significantly lower than reward learning accuracy (M = 0.87, OR = 0.50, p < .001)   - High SA group punishment learning accuracy significantly lower than low SA group punishment accuracy (M = 0.88, OR = 0.47, p = .003)   - Discriminability: SA Group x Difficulty interaction (OR = 0.99, p < .001); SA Group x Magnitude interaction (OR = 1.01, p = .001)   - High SA group showed lower accuracy on lose/lose pairs (M = 0.60) vs. low SA group (M = 0.71, OR = 0.60, p = .02)

## Quality
- **sample_size:** N = 157 (n = 114 high social anxiety, n = 43 low social anxiety); ages 18–45; largely non-Hispanic White undergraduates. Training phase data: n = 156 (1 excluded for data saving error). Testing phase data: n = 154 (1 additional excluded per group for technical issues).
- **population_category:** clinical
- **population_age_range:** 18–45
- **ecological_validity:** Low — static face photographs lack dynamic social cues (movement, sound); abstract probabilistic task with no real social interaction; stimuli limited to White faces only; outcomes (happy/angry expressions) are simplified operationalizations of social reward/punishment. Authors acknowledge these limitations explicitly.
- **eligibility_flag:** 
- **concerns:** No a priori power analysis for the specific analyses reported (sample size determined by parent study's intervention aim). Analogue sample (not clinically diagnosed social anxiety disorder). Individual-level parameter estimates had relatively wide posterior distributions, indicating uncertainty. No parameter recovery or model recovery analyses reported. Mean fitted parameter values not reported — only group-level posterior distributions described qualitatively.
- **limitations_reported:** Stimuli were static, White, facial images, which lack important social cues (e.g., movement, sound) and diversity; participants were largely non-Hispanic, White undergraduates, whose social reinforcement learning may differ from individuals at different developmental stages and those holding more marginalized identities; an a priori power analysis was not performed to determine sample size for these specific analyses; using White faces for stimuli limits understanding of social RL across the range of possible sex/gender, racial, and ethnic identities; analogue rather than clinically diagnosed sample
- **limitations_categorized:** limited ecological validity; stimulus simplicity; limited sample diversity; no power analysis; task simplicity; analogue sample
