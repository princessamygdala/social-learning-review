## Computational level
- **study_focus:** Social controllability learning; forward thinking to exploit influence over others' behavior in social exchange
- **study_focus_short:** Social controllability learning
- **learning_mode_description:** - Learning mode: Learning how one's own actions influence future offers from social partners (social controllability) via forward thinking/mental simulation   - Learning from:     - Source type (social): other (multiple partners from a team)     - Source content (non-social): outcome (monetary offer amounts; action-outcome contingency)   - Learning about:     - Target type (social): other (partners' future behavior)     - Target content (social): action/policy (how partners' offers change in response to one's accept/reject decisions — i.e., one's own influence/controllability over others)
- **task_description:** Participants played a modified ultimatum game as responder, deciding to accept or reject monetary split offers from virtual partners on two teams (40 trials each). In the Controllable condition, rejecting increased the next partner's offer and accepting decreased it (probabilistic ±$0–2); in the Uncontrollable condition, offers were random. Participants were not told which condition was which. After all trials, participants rated their perceived controllability (0–100%).
- **task_paradigm:** Ultimatum game
- **players:** Single agent (participant), multi-target (40 virtual partners per team; 2 teams)
- **n_players:** multi-target (3+)
- **partner_type:** computer (algorithmic)
- **stimuli:** Monetary split offers ($1–$9 out of $20), partner names, emotion rating scale, self-reported controllability rating
- **method:** fMRI (Study 1); behavioural replication in online sample (n=1,342, noted but not extracted as separate row to avoid double-counting with Study 2)
- **main_result:** - Participants raised offers under Controllable vs. Uncontrollable condition (mean_C = $5.9, mean_U = $4.8, t(47.45) = 4.33, P < 0.001) - Participants rejected more medium/high offers under Controllable (middle: t(47) = 5.41, P < 0.001; high: t(72.50) = 4.00, P < 0.001) - Self-reported controllability higher for Controllable (mean_C = 65.9, mean_U = 43.7, t(74.55) = 4.10, P < 0.001) - 2-step forward thinking (FT) model best explained choices vs. 0-step and model-free (DIC comparison, t(47) = −4.45, P < 0.0001 vs. 0-step) - Expected influence (δ) higher for Controllable (mean_C = 1.33, mean_U = 0.98, t(47) = 2.90, P < 0.01) - δ correlated with mean offers under Controllable (R = 0.78, P << 0.0001) - vmPFC tracked total choice values from 2-step FT model (P_FDR < 0.05, k > 50) but NOT from 0-step model - vmPFC ROI coefficients significantly > 0 for 2-step model (Controllable: t(47) = 1.96, P < 0.05 one-tailed; Uncontrollable: t(47) = 2.14, P < 0.05 one-tailed) - vmPFC signal correlated with belief-behavior disconnection under Uncontrollable (R = 0.35, P < 0.05) but not Controllable (R = −0.14, P = 0.38)

- **population_category:** healthy adults

- **ecological_validity:** Moderate. The multi-partner structure (40 per team) adds some ecological validity over dyadic games. Online replication increases generalizability.
- **eligibility_flag:** 
- **concerns:** - Participants were deceived (told playing with humans, actually computer algorithm) — social agent is automated. Flag. - Offer range was unintentionally mismatched between Controllable ($1–$9) and Uncontrollable ($2–$8) in fMRI sample. - One-tailed tests used for vmPFC ROI analyses. - γ fixed at 0.8 based on one round of estimation — sensitivity to this choice not explored. - Non-social control task (n=27) was administered 14–24 months later to a subset, limiting comparability. - Online replication sample (n=1,342) overlaps with Study 2 (Chapter 4) — potential double-counting issue.
- **limitations_reported:** Authors' own: "the question of how task-specific cognitive functioning remains intact under the Controllable condition — despite aberrant controllability beliefs in delusional individuals — is beyond the scope of the present study"; "a task that carefully controls for uncertainty and autocorrelation confounds would help better understanding the accumulative effects of social controllability"; acknowledged that autocorrelation confound made some effects marginal (P = 0.07 for expected influence; P = 0.05 for self-reported controllability when controlling for autocorrelation); acknowledged that the scope of social environment is not clearly defined; acknowledged difficulty manipulating multiple social dimensions simultaneously; noted participants were community sample, not clinical patients.
- **limitations_categorized:** Limited ecological validity; autocorrelation confound; task simplicity; deception paradigm (not real social interaction); limited generalizability (community sample only); offer range mismatch between conditions; fixed discount factor
