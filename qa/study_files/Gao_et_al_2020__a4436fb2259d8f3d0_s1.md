## Computational level
- **study_focus:** Reinforcement learning under mortality salience — how reminders of death modulate neural underpinnings (FRN, theta-band activity) of reward prediction error processing during probabilistic learning for self vs. stranger vs. no one.
- **study_focus_short:** Reinforcement learning under mortality salience
- **learning_mode_description:** - Learning mode: Learning from monetary reward/no-reward feedback about stimulus-reward associations for different beneficiaries (self, stranger, no one) under mortality salience vs. negative affect priming   - Learning from:     - Source type (non-social): world     - Source content (non-social): outcome (monetary reward / no-reward feedback)   - Learning about:     - Target type (non-social): world     - Target content (non-social): stimulus (abstract visual pattern–reward associations)  Note: The beneficiary manipulation (self/stranger/no one) introduces a social dimension to the reward recipient, but the learning itself is about stimulus–outcome contingencies, not about social agents. ## Quality
- **sample_size:** N = 60 (31 women); 30 MS group (17 women, ages 18–25, M = 20.0 ± 1.35) and 30 NA group (14 women, ages 18–24, M = 20.1 ± 1.89). 6 participants excluded from trial-by-trial FRN–PE analysis due to EEG equipment null codes.
- **population_category:** healthy adults
- **population_age_range:** 18–25
- **ecological_validity:** Low — laboratory-based paradigm using abstract symbols and verbal mortality priming. The mortality salience manipulation (reading death-related statements) is a standard TMT paradigm but is far from naturalistic survival threat. Reward learning task uses abstract visual patterns with probabilistic feedback, not real-world decision contexts. Single collectivistic (Chinese) sample limits cross-cultural generalizability.
- **eligibility_flag:** The study uses a standard Rescorla-Wagner model fitted to behavioral data during a probabilistic learning task, which satisfies computational modeling and learning-over-time criteria. However, the core learning is about non-social stimulus–reward associations — the social dimension is limited to the beneficiary of the reward (self/stranger/no one), which modulates motivation but does not make the learning itself social. The mortality salience manipulation is a contextual modulator, not a social learning mechanism. FLAG: borderline social context — the learning target is non-social (stimulus–reward mappings); the social element is only in who benefits from the reward. May not meet "learning occurs in a social context" criterion strictly.
- **concerns:** - Only one computational model tested (Rescorla-Wagner) — no model comparison performed - No parameter recovery or model recovery reported - No simulation of the model before fitting - No posterior predictive check or model validation - Fitted parameter values (α, β) not reported numerically in text (only shown in figures) - The "social" aspect is limited to reward beneficiary manipulation — the learning process itself is not social - Between-subjects design (MS vs. NA) with N = 30 per group may be underpowered for detecting subtle interactions - Chinese undergraduate sample only — cultural specificity acknowledged by authors - Model-free behavioral measures (Win-Stay, Loss-Switch) analyzed alongside but not integrated with RL model - Temperature parameter β values not reported
- **limitations_reported:** the low task difficulty of reward learning resulted in a ceiling effect of learning performance, which might explain the null effect of MS priming on individual learning rates"; "the current work only tested a sample from a collectivistic society"; "it remains unclear whether MS-induced motivation for monetary reward is weaker in those with high-independence in a society in which independence is the locally prominent cultural orientation"; "a theoretical hypothesis regarding how death-related thoughts affect early perceptual/attentional processing and cognitive evaluation in general is lacking
- **limitations_categorized:** Ceiling effect / task simplicity; limited generalizability (single cultural sample); lack of theoretical framework for MS–cognition link; between-subjects design limitations
