## Computational level
- **study_focus:** Fear-relevant stimulus biasing of instrumental avoidance learning; Pavlovian-instrumental interaction in the context of fear-relevant social and non-social stimuli (threatening faces, out-group faces, snakes, guns).
- **study_focus_short:** Fear-relevant stimulus biasing of instrumental avoidance learning
- **learning_mode_description:** - Learning mode: Learning from aversive outcomes (electric shock) about which stimulus to choose (avoid) to minimize danger, with Pavlovian bias from fear-relevant stimuli competing with instrumental learning.   - Learning from:     - Source type (non-social): world       - Source content (non-social): outcome (electric shock; aversive reinforcement)     - Source type (social/non-social, depending on experiment): self (own choice outcomes)       - Source content (non-social): outcome   - Learning about:     - Target type (non-social): world (stimulus-action values)       - Target content (non-social): action/policy (which stimulus to select to avoid shock)     - Target type (social, Experiments 1 & 4): other (threatening in-group face; racial out-group face)       - Target content (social): stimulus (Pavlovian value of fear-relevant social stimulus)  Note: The task is primarily non-social instrumental avoidance learning. The "social" element is limited to the *stimuli* used (threatening faces in Exp 1, out-group racial faces in Exp 4). The learning itself -- updating action values from shock outcomes -- is non-social. The Pavlovian bias from fear-relevant stimuli (including social stimuli) is not learned within the task but is pre-existing.
- **task_description:** Participants performed a two-choice decision task (70 trials) selecting between a fear-relevant stimulus and a neutral stimulus, receiving probabilistic aversive reinforcement (electric shock). After 35 trials, stimulus-reinforcement contingencies reversed. Participants were assigned to reliable-to-unreliable or unreliable-to-reliable groups.

- **players:** Single agent (participant), no social interaction partner. Four between-subjects experiments varying stimuli: Exp 1 (threatening vs. happy faces), Exp 2 (snakes vs. flowers), Exp 3 (guns vs. flowers), Exp 4 (out-group vs. in-group faces). Two additional independent rating samples.

## Quality
- **sample_size:** N = 156 across 4 experiments (Exp 1: N=42, 22F, mean age 26; Exp 2: N=32, 21F, mean age 22; Exp 3: N=34, 18F, mean age 25; Exp 4: N=48, 34F, mean age 26). Two additional rating samples: N=59 (39F) and N=35 (26F).
- **population_category:** healthy adults
- **population_age_range:** M=26
- **ecological_validity:** Lab-based forced-choice task with electric shock reinforcement. Stimuli include ecologically relevant fear-relevant images (faces, snakes, guns). However, the binary choice task with artificial shock contingencies has limited ecological validity for real-world fear-driven avoidance. Authors note the design is "directly relevant to real-world situations" where Pavlovian and instrumental value co-occur (e.g., interacting with someone whose facial features trigger the Pavlovian system), but the task itself is highly simplified.
- **eligibility_flag:** FLAG -- Borderline social learning. The task is fundamentally a non-social instrumental avoidance learning task. The "social" element is limited to the stimulus category used in 2 of 4 experiments (threatening faces, out-group faces). Learning does not occur in a social context (no social interaction, no social information source, no social inference). The Pavlovian bias from social stimuli is pre-existing and not learned within the task. This paper is primarily about Pavlovian-instrumental interaction with fear-relevant stimuli, not social learning per se.
- **concerns:** - Supplement (OSMs) not accessible -- contains full model equations for all alternative models, Table S1 (simple effect sizes), Table S2 (full model comparison), additional parameter analyses, and trial-by-trial plots. Without these, model details are incomplete. - Only 2 of 4 experiments use social stimuli; the other 2 use non-social stimuli (snakes, guns). - The "social" aspect is limited to stimulus category, not the learning process itself. - No parameter recovery or model recovery reported (at least not in main text; possibly in supplement). - Mean fitted parameter values for alpha and beta not reported in main text. - Individual differences analysis for out-group faces relies on median split of explicit racial bias, which has well-known statistical limitations. - Post-hoc stimulus ratings from a separate sample used for qualitative (not quantitative) comparison with experimental results.
- **limitations_reported:** Authors note: inability to identify validated Swedish scales for fear/negative attitudes toward threatening faces or guns, limiting individual difference analyses; the relationship between individual differences in attitudes and Pavlovian parameter remains unknown for threatening faces and guns; post-experiment ratings may be confounded by conditioning effects during the experiment; comparison between ratings and experimental results could only be qualitative; implicit racial bias (IAT) did not show the same pattern as explicit racial bias.
- **limitations_categorized:** Limited individual difference measures; potential confound in post-experiment ratings; qualitative-only cross-sample comparison; limited generalizability of individual difference findings.
