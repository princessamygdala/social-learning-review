- **study_focus:** Observational causal learning from emotional displays; reasoning about others' beliefs/desires/knowledge from emotions to make causal inferences about the world
- **study_focus_short:** Observational causal learning from emotional displays
- **learning_mode_description:** - Learning mode: Learning causal relationships about the world by observing another agent's actions and emotional displays (frustration, happiness)     - Learning from:       - Source type (social): other (observed agent)         - Not joint       - Source content (social): action/policy + state (emotional displays conveying beliefs/desires/knowledge)         - Not joint     - Learning about:       - Target type (non-social): world (causal structure of environment -- which switches cause which outcomes)         - Not joint       - Target content (non-social): state (world causal structure)         - Not joint
- **task_description:** Participants watched videos of an agent interacting with switches and light bulbs. In Study 1, the agent pushed two switches sequentially and the bulb lit up only after the second; participants inferred which switch(es) caused the outcome. In Study 2, the agent pushed one switch near two bulbs and only one lit up; participants inferred causal relationships. Emotional displays (frustration/happiness) were manipulated between conditions.
- **task_paradigm:** Observational learning task
- **players:** Single agent (participant/observer), single target (observed agent in video)
- **n_players:** single agent (1)
- **partner_type:** human (recorded)
- **stimuli:** Animated videos of an agent, colored switches (blue, orange), light bulbs (pink, orange); emotional expressions (frustrated, happy, neutral); Likert-scale rating prompts
- **method:** online / behavioural
- **method_full:** behavioural (online)
- **main_result:** - Study 1a: Emotion condition led participants to discount blue switch as causal (W = 2924.0) and attribute different beliefs to the agent (W = 1028.5 and W = 3055.5)   - Study 1a: Model-human correlation across World, Belief, Desire, Knowledge: r(14) = .883   - Study 1b: Model-human dynamic causal inference correlation across 5 timepoints: r(28) = .746   - Study 2: Model-human correlation: r(12) = .918   - Lesioning emotions: correlation dropped from .91 to .67 (Fisher's Z, p = .045)   - Lesioning knowledgeability: correlation dropped from .91 to .67 (p = .043)

## Quality
- **sample_size:** Study 1a: N=129 (59 emotion, 70 no-emotion; 41.9% female, M_age=28.8, SD=9.2); Study 1b: N=127 (58 emotion, 69 no-emotion; 44.9% female, M_age=26.1, SD=8.7); Study 2: N=125 (68 emotion, 57 no-emotion; 40.1% female, M_age=28.1, SD=11.0). Total N=381 across 3 experiments.
- **population_category:** healthy adults
- **population_age_range:** 
- **ecological_validity:** Low-moderate; animated video stimuli with stylized agents and simple mechanical devices; between-subjects online study; limited naturalistic social interaction
- **eligibility_flag:** Borderline -- causal inference task, not clear temporal learning over multiple trials (participants observe a single scenario and make one-shot inferences; Study 1b measures at 5 timepoints within a single video but this is within-trial dynamic updating, not trial-by-trial learning). Flag: possible one-shot inference rather than learning over time.
- **concerns:** (1) The model has no free parameters fitted to individual participants -- all priors are fixed or calibrated from a separate sample (Study 1b MP1). This is more of a "computational-level" rational analysis than a process model with fitted parameters. (2) Model comparison is only via correlation between model predictions and group-mean human ratings, not formal model comparison (BIC/AIC). (3) The task involves single-scenario observation, not repeated trial-by-trial learning -- borderline for "learning over time" inclusion criterion. (4) Conference proceedings paper (6 pages), so many details are necessarily abbreviated.
- **limitations_reported:** Model's treatment of emotions is simple, only considering goal-related appraisals leading to frustration and happiness; other emotions like surprise or excitement not considered; contextual factors not modeled (frustration could signal knowledge in other contexts); model made more polarized inferences than humans in no-emotion conditions, suggesting people are less able to make inferences from absence of outcome alone
- **limitations_categorized:** model simplicity; limited emotion types; limited contextual factors; model-human discrepancy in no-emotion condition; task simplicity
